ODA: what the residency clause actually means

Chevening is paid for largely out of the UK's aid budget. That is the whole reason this clause exists — and it is why the rule is about where you live and where you will go back to, not only about your passport.

ODA = Official Development Assistance

It is the OECD's technical term for government aid, defined by its Development Assistance Committee (the DAC) in 1969 and still the international standard for what counts as aid. A flow of money is ODA only if it meets three conditions:

  • it comes from the official sector — a government, a local authority, or their executive agencies;
  • its main objective is the economic development and welfare of developing countries;
  • it is concessional — a grant, or a loan with a grant element of at least 25%.

A UK government scholarship that trains someone who then returns home to work on their country's development fits that definition. A scholarship for someone who will settle abroad does not — hence the two-year return commitment.

An "ODA-eligible country" is one on the DAC List of ODA Recipients

The OECD publishes a list of every country and territory that may receive ODA. Membership is not political: it is set by World Bank gross national income per capita, reviewed every three years. A country leaves the list once it has been above the World Bank's high-income threshold for three consecutive years. The list sorts countries into four groups:

Egypt is on the list, in the Lower Middle Income group. Your citizenship therefore clears this criterion. The question in your case is the second half of the clause — where you are resident.

Your situation: Egyptian citizen, Saudi Premium Residency, living in Riyadh

Saudi Arabia is not on the DAC List of ODA Recipients. Nor are the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain or Oman — the Gulf states are high-income and long since graduated off the list. The Middle Eastern countries that are on it are Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, Iran, Syria, Iraq, Yemen and the West Bank and Gaza Strip.

Chevening's clause reads: "If applying for an award from an ODA-eligible country, be resident in an ODA-eligible country." Egypt is an ODA-eligible country, so you would be applying for the Egypt award — and the rule then requires your country of residence to be on the list too. Saudi Arabia is not. On the criterion as written, residence in Saudi Arabia does not satisfy it, and the FAQ's own worked example is exactly this shape: an Egyptian citizen living in the USA is not permitted, because the USA is not ODA-eligible.

Your Premium Residency makes this harder rather than easier to argue. A two-year expatriate posting can be presented as temporary; permanent residency reads as settled residence outside the ODA-eligible world, and it is a document you would have to declare.

Before you accept that as final, do two things. Residence is assessed at the time of application, so the answer can turn on where you are on 6 October 2026 and on how your situation is documented. First, write to the Chevening team in Cairo through chevening.org/about/contact-us and describe your circumstances plainly — Egyptian citizenship, Saudi Premium Residency, and the Presidential Labor Medal awarded by Egypt in May 2025 for work that served Egyptian as well as Saudi interests. That last fact is worth stating: it is the strongest available evidence that your orientation is toward Egypt. A binding answer comes only from them. Second, do not spend your eligibility-checker attempts guessing: you get four, and the checker asks for your country of residence.

The general rule: residence ≠ citizenship

Allowed

An Egyptian citizen living and working in Jordan, Algeria, Morocco or another country on the DAC list. Citizenship country and country of residence are both ODA-eligible, so you can apply; you explain the circumstances in the application, and your country of award stays Egypt.

Not allowed

An Egyptian citizen living in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, the USA or the UK. All are high-income and off the DAC list, so residence there does not meet the criterion — and UK residence is separately disqualifying in its own right.

Choosing the three courses

Three is a rule, not a suggestion, and the choices are themselves scored — assessors read them as evidence of how well you understand your own career.

A course qualifies if it is

  • Full-time
  • Starting in the autumn term — usually September or October 2027
  • A taught master's leading to a master's qualification
  • Based in the UK
  • Between 9 and 12 months long

It does not qualify if it is

  • Part-time or distance learning
  • Shorter than 9 months or longer than 12
  • A PhD, DPhil, or a research-focused MRes
  • Requiring more than one month of study outside the UK
  • Preceded by a pre-sessional course — taking even an optional one costs you the scholarship

The fine print that catches people

  • Three different courses. Submitting the same course twice, or duplicating a university where the rules don't allow it, is a listed common error.
  • MBA fee cap of £22,000. Chevening pays up to that; the difference is yours to find, and UK MBA fees routinely exceed it by a wide margin.
  • London campuses of non-London universities are only allowed when the same course isn't offered at the main campus.
  • Order matters. List them in genuine order of preference — you will be asked about the ranking at interview.
  • Check each one in Chevening's course finder before you commit; it is the only way to confirm a specific course's eligibility.

A sensible strategy

Either pick three closely related courses at three different universities — which maximises the chance that at least one unconditional offer lands by July 2027 — or three complementary courses at one university you are certain about. What does not work is three unrelated subjects: it reads as someone who has not decided what they want, and the "Studying in the UK" essay becomes impossible to write convincingly.

Four essays and two referees

The reading committee sees no interview, no CV narrative and no charm — only these four answers. Each is scored against one criterion, and each is capped at roughly 500 words (a 100-word minimum applies; the exact counter is shown in the form).

1 · Leadership and influence

Describe times you have demonstrated leadership and influenced others.

What fails: defining what leadership means to you. What works: two or three episodes built on STAR — situation, task, action, result — where the action is yours in the first person singular and the result carries a number. Reading committees report that "we" and "the team" are the commonest way strong candidates make themselves invisible.

2 · Networking and relationship building

Describe how you build and maintain relationships professionally.

What fails: conference attendance, LinkedIn follower counts, generic claims about "being a people person". What works: a specific relationship you built across a boundary — sector, institution, discipline, country — what it produced, and how you still use it. Then say what you intend to build in the UK, concretely.

3 · Studying in the UK

Why these three courses, and how do they connect your background to your plan?

What fails: not naming the universities, or prose lifted from a prospectus. What works: a distinct paragraph per course naming modules, a research centre or a named academic, and the specific gap in your own expertise it closes. Ranked, with a reason for the ranking.

4 · Career plan

What are your short-, medium- and long-term career goals, and how will the scholarship help you achieve them?

What fails: aspiration without a route, and a stated plan to do a PhD with no research interest behind it. What works: one concrete first role in Egypt on return, a defensible five-year step, a long-term contribution to a named problem, and a line that links it to one of Chevening's impact areas.

AI-written essays are grounds for rejection

Chevening's guidance is explicit: using AI software to generate answers to the essay questions is strictly prohibited, and presenting AI-generated content as your own is treated alongside plagiarism and fraud — automatic disqualification. Use tools to plan, to check grammar, and to rehearse; write the answers yourself, in your own voice, from your own evidence.

Referees

  • Two referees — two professional, two academic, or one of each. Never a relative or a close personal friend.
  • At application you supply only their names, jobs and contact details.
  • Letters are needed only if you are invited to interview, and must reach the Chevening team no later than seven working days before the interview. Late means your application is no longer considered.
  • In English, addressed to the British Embassy in Cairo, uploaded by you as two separate files — Chevening will not chase your referees.
  • Each letter should state how long and in what capacity they have known you, when they last had contact, and speak to your leadership, networking, interpersonal skills and academic ability.
  • Arabic letters need the original plus a notarised English translation in a single file.
  • You may change referees after being shortlisted, but expect to be asked why at interview.

Worth knowing about Egypt's programme

Chevening does not publish an annual award count for Egypt, and it does not restrict Egyptian applicants to particular subjects — the scholarship is open in any subject. Egypt has one of the longer-running programmes in the region, with alumni including Mahmoud El Sakka, a 1990 scholar who went on to advise the Egyptian Prime Minister. Chevening's partner awards — co-funded scholarships with universities and organisations — sometimes attract fewer applicants than the main award; it is worth checking the partner list when you choose your courses.
